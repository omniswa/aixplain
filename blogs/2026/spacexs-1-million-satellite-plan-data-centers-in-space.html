SpaceX’s 1 Million Satellite Plan: Data Centers in Space

The FCC just approved SpaceX’s plan for 1 million satellites to run AI data centers in orbit. Here’s what’s real, what’s hype, and what worries scientists.

By xclnt-sensai | Published Nov 27, 2026 | 4-minute read

On Wednesday, a US regulator was handed a proposal to park a million computers in orbit, and it said yes. Not “let’s study it” or “come back with a smaller number.” Yes.

The cloud has officially left the atmosphere. The physics, as we’ll see, did not get the memo.

What the FCC actually approved

SpaceX applied on January 30, 2026 for a satellite system of up to one million satellites. This week, the FCC authorized what it calls “orbital compute modules,” forming a data-center constellation named Starmind. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said America is leading the world into the era of superintelligence. The agency also called the approval a historic first.

For scale, SpaceX’s existing network has grown to just over 11,000 satellites. A million is roughly 90 times that (my math, not Reuters’).

Before anyone cancels their evening plans, note the schedule. SpaceX must deploy at least one authorized space station by 2033, then gradually add more through 2040. Authorized is not the same as built. A permit slip is not a launch pad.

Why would anyone do this?

Because AI is hungry. Data centers on Earth eat electricity and drink water, and communities are noticing. The pitch from Musk and company is that space has near-constant sunshine, no zoning board and no neighbors complaining about the hum.

Proponents argue that capturing the sun’s energy would ease pressure on power grids and cut demand for sprawling computing warehouses and the water needed to cool them. SpaceX’s own filing, as Fortune reported, framed it as a first step toward becoming a Kardashev II-level civilization, meaning one that harnesses the full power of its star.

You have to admire the ambition. Most companies’ five-year plans don’t include a rung on the cosmic civilization ladder.

The thermos problem

Here’s where the plot twist arrives. Space is cold, which sounds ideal for chips that run hot. But space is also a vacuum, which traps heat inside objects much like a thermos.

You know what a thermos is great at? Keeping hot things hot. On Earth, a data center sheds heat with air and water. In a vacuum there’s no air, so you can only radiate heat away, and that doesn’t go as quickly as the brochure suggests.

Astrophysicist Adam Becker says there are basic physical limits to radiating that heat, and he called the idea “a fantasy.” He’s not alone in his skepticism, though the FCC’s job was to rule on licensing, not on whether the engineering works out.

The traffic jam and the missing stars

Then there’s the crowding. Critics warn of in-orbit collisions that could set off a cascading set of space-junk crashes, putting weather and telecom satellites at risk in low Earth orbit. Launches and reentries add atmospheric pollution to the list.

The American Astronomical Society said it was “disheartened and dismayed,” arguing that larger, brighter and more numerous objects would undercut federal investment in astronomy and spoil the night sky. Viasat and Amazon’s Kuiper Systems also urged the FCC to deny the request.

The FCC’s response, in short, was that the constellation fits its orbital debris rules and serves the public interest. It also set aside topics outside its authority, such as environmental concerns. That’s a tidy way to approve a million objects: the sky’s problems are simply someone else’s department.

Back-of-napkin math, for fun

Suppose SpaceX wanted all one million up by 2040, starting in 2033. That’s about 390 satellites a day, every day, for seven years. That’s my arithmetic, and the FCC doesn’t require anything of the sort. But it shows why launch cadence, money and manufacturing matter as much as the license.

So is this real?

Partly. The approval is real. The legal green light is real. The physics problem, the financing and the astronomers’ objections are also very real.

Here’s what I’d watch:

  1. The 2033 deadline. One working orbital data center is a bigger story than a million licensed ones.
  2. The heat question. If SpaceX has a cooling answer, we haven’t seen it. If it has a clever one, that’s the headline.
  3. The night-sky fight. Astronomers have the data and the patience. Expect this to keep going.
  4. Copycats. If the FCC has blessed orbital data centers once, other companies will notice.

The bottom line

Skeptics may be right that this is a fantasy. Boosters may be right that the skeptics said the same about reusable rockets. We’ll know more by 2033, when SpaceX needs at least one of these things working.

A million satellites is a big number. The number that matters is one: the first station, due by 2033. If it works, the rest is logistics. If it doesn’t, we’ll have built the most expensive thermos in history.
